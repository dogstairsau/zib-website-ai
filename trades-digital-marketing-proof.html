What two Australian trades businesses teach us about building demand that compounds.

Their results are not a benchmark or a guarantee. What they offer is more useful: evidence of the commercial principles that matter when a local service business wants to become a stronger one.

CK Chris Knights 19 July 2026 6 min read
Proof sharpens the question.

Trades marketing is crowded with easy promises. More leads. Better rankings. A full calendar. A cheaper cost per click.

The claims are simple because the real job is not.

A trades business needs demand it can service, a website that turns urgency into confidence, a response process that protects the opportunity and a reporting loop that connects marketing to work won.

Two recent businesses in Zib's public case-study library show how those parts can work together: Outback Fencing and a Brisbane roof restoration and repair company.

Their results are not a benchmark or a guarantee. The businesses, markets and timeframes are different. What they offer is something more useful: evidence of the commercial principles that tend to matter when a local service business wants to become a stronger one.

Proof case 1: Outback Fencing rebuilt the asset before chasing the demand

Outback Fencing sells fencing products online, which made the organic challenge harder: an ecommerce site that was poorly set up to begin with, in a category where content is not an easy win.

The program did not start with more traffic. It started with the foundation. The site was restructured, the organic base rebuilt, and only then did the work drive hard at the categories that mattered commercially.

The public case study reports a 309% increase in ecommerce transactions from organic traffic in a short period, with organic sessions up 80% and total impressions in Australia up 27%.

The ordering is the lesson. Transactions grew because the asset could finally support them. A weak site with more traffic is still a weak site.

Commercial lesson: fix the conversion asset before scaling demand into it. And judge organic search by what it sells, not where it ranks.

Proof case 2: a Brisbane roofing business bought demand against a revenue target

A Brisbane-based roof restoration and repair company needed a consistent pipeline of qualified leads across roof repairs, restorations, cleaning and painting to support ambitious revenue growth in a competitive trades market.

The answer was a dual-channel paid strategy: Google Ads structured around core service categories to capture high-intent search, and Meta using video and before-and-after creative to build awareness and generate leads at scale.

Nine months in, the public case study reports the business on track to grow from $2.8 million to $5.3 million in revenue driven by paid advertising, with 2,671 conversions tracked across the program: 1,095 phone calls, 518 form submissions and 449 quote requests. On the search side, Google Ads delivered 461 conversions at $27.94 per lead.

That is the crucial bridge between marketing evidence and commercial evidence. The campaign was not judged on clicks or impressions. Every conversion type was tracked to something the owner cares about, and the whole program answered to a revenue number.

Commercial lesson: give paid media a revenue brief, then track calls, forms and quote requests separately. A form fill and a quote request are not the same thing, and the campaign should know the difference.

The pattern across both cases

Outback Fencing sells products. The roofing business sells trades services. One led with organic. One led with paid. The repeatable pattern is not a tactic; it is the shape of the system.

1. The commercial outcome came first

One brief was anchored to ecommerce transactions, the other to a revenue target. Visibility was a means, not the finish line. For another trades operator, the outcome might be a better mix of jobs, entry into an adjacent region, steadier winter demand or less dependence on one lead source. That choice should be made before channel budgets are set.

2. The asset earned the demand

Outback Fencing's growth required rebuilding the site's organic foundation first. The roofing campaign worked because clicks landed somewhere that could turn urgency into calls and quote requests. In both cases, the website was treated as part of the acquisition system, not a brochure sitting beside it.

3. Search demand was captured at more than one speed

Paid search can meet active need now. Organic search can build a durable position around the services and categories the business wants to own. The balance will change with capacity, competition and seasonality. The system should be able to change with it.

4. Measurement made the results commercial

Transactions from organic. Calls, forms and quote requests from paid. Revenue as the scoreboard. When reporting is built this way, leadership can decide each week whether to scale, reshape or stop, using evidence that matters. That is the core of Zib's diagnose, plan, execute and report methodology.

What another trades business should do with this proof

Do not copy the channel split or expect the same result. Use the cases to ask better questions:

  • Which service lines should marketing grow, and which should it stop feeding?
  • Where can crews work profitably?
  • What makes an enquiry genuinely qualified?
  • Is the website specific enough to turn a local problem into confidence?
  • Are paid and organic search sharing information?
  • Can the CRM connect source, service, quote and won work?
  • Is reporting helping leadership make a decision each week?

That is how case-study proof becomes commercially useful. It gives the next business a sharper diagnostic, not a borrowed promise.

Proof is the start of the conversation

Outback Fencing shows the value of rebuilding the owned asset and judging organic search by transactions. The Brisbane roofing business shows the power of buying demand against a revenue target with every enquiry type tracked.

Neither case says there is one formula for Australian trades. Together, they say something more credible: when demand, trust, conversion and measurement are run through one commercial methodology, marketing can become part of how the business grows, not just how it advertises.
