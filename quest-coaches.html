The three things that matter

You need a page for each kind of job. A school books an excursion. A club books an away game. A winery books guest transport. Those are three different people asking three different questions, and right now they all land on a page that shows them a list of buses. That's also why you've slipped off Google: there was never a page strong enough to hold its spot once other operators put the work in.

Your phone number doesn't work on a phone. Most people looking for a coach are on their mobile. On yours, the 1300 number is just text — they can't tap it to call. It's the cheapest thing on this list to fix and the one costing you the most.

You can't see where your work comes from. Your visitor tracking stopped collecting anything in 2023, and quotes happen on a separate booking website. So nobody can tell you which suburb, which service or which search brought last month's bookings in.

The good news

There's no penalty and nothing to appeal. Google hasn't punished you. You've just been out-built. Two of the four operators we looked at haven't bothered either, and nobody at all has built pages for footy clubs or for wineries. There's room here.

Your phone number

Someone on their phone can't call you.

This gets its own section because it's the clearest money you're losing, and it's a half-hour fix.

Your whole website points at the 1300 number. On a computer, fine. On a phone, two things break it.

Normally a phone spots a number on a website and turns it into a button you can tap to call. Your site specifically tells it not to do that. And there's no tap-to-call link anywhere to make up for it. So the number sits there as plain text. To ring you, someone has to memorise it, leave your website and dial it manually. Plenty won't bother.

On top of that, your site was built for a computer screen and never updated for phones. It loads as a shrunk-down desktop page, so that number is about half the size it should be while they're trying to read it.

Why it keeps slipping

Google works out how established a business is partly from how many other websites link to yours. Yours has three. One is a genuine bus industry site worth building on. The other two are the sort of junk directory that counts for nothing.

Your most important page also has about 296 words on it. Crown's main school page has 2,276. When Google had to choose, you had nothing to hold your place with.

You've also stopped turning up for searches on your own name — you used to show for eleven of those.

The one search you still win proves the point

The last one standing is “coach charter” — 150 searches a month. You've gone from 58th to 9th on it. And that's by accident: it's a phrase that happens to appear on your homepage. One accidental mention moved you up 49 places. That's what a page built on purpose is meant to do, across thirty searches instead of one.

One more thing, and it's costing you school work

Under Victorian education rules, a school booking a private coach has to satisfy itself that the operator is properly accredited. Your website gives them nothing to check. Your about page says “fully accredited” and leaves it there — no accreditation number, nothing about seatbelts, no child safety policy, and no mention of your drivers' Working With Children checks.

A school business manager comparing three quotes will find all of that on Crown's website and none of it on yours. Even Nuline, thin as their site is, publishes a child safety policy. Your site still has a COVID notice on every page and a footer that says 2021.

Three short pages fix this, and they're in the first stage of the build for that reason.

How pages get built

Three page recipes, thirty-six pages.

Building 36 pages from scratch one at a time would cost a fortune. Instead we design three layouts once, then fill each page with its own words, photos, questions and reviews. Same shape, different content every time.

Recipe 1

Trip pages

23 pages. One per kind of job — school camps, away games, wine tours, conferences.

  1. A headline naming the exact job and the city
  2. Tap-to-call button and a three-question quote form, before they scroll
  3. Who it's for, in words that customer would use
  4. What's included and what costs extra, up front
  5. Which bus suits which group size
  6. The proof that customer cares about — accreditation for schools, invoicing for companies, seatbelts for clubs
  7. Two or three real trips of that kind you've done
  8. Which areas you cover for it
  9. Four to six questions and answers
  10. A review from that kind of customer

About 900 to 1,400 words each.

Recipe 2

Area pages

9 pages. One per part of Melbourne or Victoria you actually run to.

  1. A headline naming the area, and how far it is from your depot
  2. Tap-to-call button and quote form
  3. How you serve it — pickup points, roads, how long a run takes
  4. The suburbs and towns covered, written as a sentence
  5. Places you actually drive to from there
  6. The jobs you get booked for most in that area
  7. One local job described properly, with a review if we have one
  8. Three questions: travel time, minimum hire, out-of-area cost
  9. Map and depot details

If we can't write 300 words that could only be about that area, we don't build the page.

Recipe 3

Bus pages

4 pages. One per vehicle, named the way people search: 25-seater, coaster, mini bus.

  1. A headline with the seat count
  2. Real photos of that bus, inside and out
  3. The details: seats, seatbelts, air con, PA, luggage space, trailer option, accessibility
  4. What it's right for, and when to go bigger or smaller
  5. The trips it gets booked for most
  6. Said plainly: no toilet on board, and what that means for long runs
  7. Quote form and phone number

About 500 to 700 words each. These catch the size searches you were already turning up for.

Why we don't just copy and paste

The layout repeats. The words never do. Google has spent the last few years getting good at spotting pages that are the same thing with the nouns swapped, and it buries them. The words, photos, venues, questions and reviews get written per page from what you actually do. That's the difference between 36 pages that earn work and 36 that drag you down, and it's why this is spread over twelve months rather than dumped online in a week.

What we'll count, and what we won't promise

Four things get counted from day one, and they're the ones your office would recognise: someone tapped the phone number, someone filled in the quote form on your site, someone started a quote in your booking system, and someone emailed you. Each one gets traced back to the page and the search that brought them, which is what makes these new pages accountable instead of decorative.

What we won't do is promise you a traffic number today. You've had no working data since 2023, and the industry tool we're using is estimating from four visitors a month, which is too small to be reliable. We set the targets at the end of the first month against real data, and review them every three. Any agency quoting you visitor numbers for this site right now is guessing.

What is fair to say: you currently compete for one search. This plan aims you at searches with real, published demand, including ten you were already turning up for a year ago. Getting back to where you were last August isn't the goal — it's the floor.
