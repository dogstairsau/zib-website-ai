Summary

The site isn't broken. It was never built to sell.

Every page loads. Nothing 404s. That is the whole of the good news. What Quest has is a 2016-era brochure served off unsupported software, with no page for any of the four markets that actually pay the invoices, no way for a phone user to tap the 1300 number, and no working analytics since the middle of 2023.

The three things that matter

Build the pages that match how buyers search. A school books an excursion coach. A club books an away game. A winery books guest transport. Each of those is a different search, a different objection and a different page. Quest currently answers all of them with a fleet list, and that is the direct reason the rankings went: there was never a page strong enough to hold “bus charter Melbourne” once competitors invested.

Fix the phone. A charter business lives on its 1300 number, and on mobile that number cannot be tapped because the site tells the browser not to make it a link. This is the cheapest fix on the list and the one costing the most money.

Get the measurement back. Quotes are handled on a separate booking portal with no shared tracking, and site analytics stopped reporting more than three years ago. Nobody can say which suburb, service or search produced last month's bookings.

What this document is

Two scopes in one place. Sections 5 to 8 are the website: the site map, the three page templates and the rebuild of what exists. Sections 7 and 9 to 12 are search: the Ahrefs audit, the technical and content work, the measurement and the 12-month sequence.

1 · Current state

What the audit found.

Crawled live on 18 September 2026 across all eleven pages. Ordered by commercial damage, not by technical severity.

Critical

The site is not responsive

Every page declares width=550 as its viewport instead of the device width. A phone renders the desktop layout shrunk to fit, so text is unreadable until the visitor pinches and zooms. Most charter enquiries start on a phone.

Critical

Tap-to-call is switched off

Every page carries format-detection: telephone=no, and there is not a single tel: link anywhere on the site. The 1300 number is plain text that a phone will not dial. Crown and Ventura both put two tappable numbers on every page.

Critical

No page for any market Quest serves

Schools, corporate, sporting clubs, weddings, wineries, seniors and airport work are named in a list on the homepage and the about page. None has a page. There is nothing for Google to rank and nothing for a buyer to read.

Critical

No analytics since mid-2023

The only tag installed is Universal Analytics (UA-90737186-1), which stopped processing data in July 2023. No GA4, no Tag Manager, no conversion tracking, no call tracking.

Critical

Quoting happens on a domain with no tracking

The quote link leaves the site for portal.questcoaches.com.au, a Coach Manager booking portal. Without cross-domain measurement, every quote that starts on the website is invisible, and a 20-plus field form is the only path offered.

Critical

Zero structured data

No LocalBusiness, no Service, no FAQPage, no review markup — across all eleven pages. The FAQ page and eight testimonials are both sitting there unmarked, which is the easiest schema win on the site.

Major

No robots.txt and no sitemap.xml

Both return 404. Nothing directs a crawler, and there is no sitemap to submit to Search Console. Crown publishes four segmented sitemaps.

Major

Two <title> tags per page

Every page declares its title twice. The second contains a typo — “Mebourne” — and Google may use either. The pages also still carry a meta keywords tag, which has been ignored since 2009.

Major

www and non-www both resolve, with no canonical

Both hosts return 200 with different content lengths and no canonical tag anywhere on the site. The http redirect is a 302, not a 301. Google is left to pick a version.

Major

Unsupported server software

Pages are served by PHP 5.6, which has had no security patches since December 2018, alongside jQuery 1.11 from 2014. HTML responses are also sent no-store, so nothing caches.

Major

Thin content throughout

The homepage carries 349 words; the fleet page 196; about 215. Crown's two main service pages carry 1,789 and 2,276. There is not enough on any Quest page to compete for a commercial search.

Critical

A competitor's brand name in Quest's page title

The terms page is titled “Crown Bus Charter”. It is the reason Quest turns up for crown buses and crown coaches bus — the site is helping a direct competitor's brand searches. The credit card terms page then duplicates that same title.

Major

Missing titles, descriptions and a double H1

The COVID-19 page has no title and no meta description. The quote page is titled only “Quest Coaches” and carries two H1s. Both terms pages have no description. Apart from the homepage, no H1 on the site contains a keyword anyone searches.

Major

The site reads as abandoned

A COVID-19 banner still sits on every page, six years on, with its own page still linked sitewide. The footer reads © 2021. For a buyer checking whether an operator is still trading, that is the first impression.

Moderate

No sharing previews, no page language

No Open Graph tags, so a link posted to Facebook or sent through Messenger renders as a bare URL with no image. No lang attribute either, which is a basic accessibility and localisation signal.

Moderate

A broken heading, and one heavy image

The homepage H1 reads “Reliable and ProfessionalBus Charter Service” — a missing space from a line break inside the heading. Load time is otherwise fine at roughly two seconds; the largest single asset is a 418 KB JPEG that should be a modern format.

2 · The mobile problem

A charter business whose phone number can't be tapped.

This is worth its own section because it is the single clearest loss on the site, and it is two lines of code.

Quest's entire conversion strategy is the 1300 number. It sits in the header of every page. On a desktop that is fine. On a phone, two settings break it.

First, the site tells the browser not to turn phone numbers into links (format-detection: telephone=no). Browsers normally do that automatically, and this switches it off. Second, no tel: link exists anywhere in the markup to replace it. So the number is text. A caller has to memorise it, leave the site and dial manually.

On top of that the page is rendered at a fixed 550 pixels and scaled down, so that number is roughly half the size it should be while the visitor is trying to read it.

The rebuild puts a tappable number in the header, a sticky call-and-quote bar on mobile, and a short on-site quote form ahead of the full portal itinerary.

The fix, in order

Remove the phone-detection block. Add tel:1300255287 to the header, the footer and every service page. Add a sticky mobile bar with Call and Get a quote. Then track both as conversions, so the next time we talk about this we are talking about counted calls.

3 · Flying blind

Three years with no data, and a quote form on another domain.

Any conversation about return on marketing needs a baseline. Right now there isn't one.

What is installed

One tag, on all eleven pages: Universal Analytics, property UA-90737186-1, loaded through the old analytics.js library. Google switched that platform off in July 2023. It has collected nothing since.

There is no GA4 property tagged on the site, no Google Tag Manager container, no Google Ads conversion tag and no Meta pixel. There is also no call tracking, so a phone enquiry leaves no record of what the caller was looking at.

The portal gap

Quoting runs on portal.questcoaches.com.au, a Coach Manager system by Distinctive Systems. That is a sound operational tool and we are not proposing to replace it. The problem is the handoff: a visitor crossing from the website to the portal starts a new session on a different host, so even with GA4 installed tomorrow, every quote would be attributed to the portal rather than to the search, page or campaign that produced it.

Cross-domain measurement across both hosts is part of the scope, along with a short qualifying form on the website itself for the majority of enquirers who will not complete a twenty-field itinerary on a phone.

To confirm

Two brand questions for Quest

The site ranks for crown terms because a page title carries a competitor's name. It also picks up quinces coaches searches. We need to know whether that is a former trading name, a partner operator, or a leftover from an old template.

Why it fell, honestly

The sharp drop lines up roughly with Google's August 2025 spam update, and it would be easy to sell that as the villain. It probably isn't. There is no sign of a penalty: the pages are indexed, they load, nothing is cloaked or spun. What the site has is 296 words on its most important page, no service pages, no schema, three referring domains and a domain rating of 0.3.

Against that, the two operators Ahrefs lists as Quest's closest competitors rank for 527 and 103 keywords. When the algorithm re-weighted, Quest had nothing to hold its positions with. Rebuilding the pages is the fix, and it is a fix we control.

What Crown has that Quest doesn't

Crown is the benchmark for schools. Their school page runs 2,276 words and does the work a decision-maker needs: 150 schools named as clients, 75 daily runs, 1,500 students, a parent tracking app, the actual camps and excursion venues Melbourne schools go to, a size-to-group comparison table, and six FAQs marked up as schema. Sub-pages sit beneath it for airport, weddings, corporate, accessible and seniors work. They publish an absentee form, a child safety policy, a bus safety policy and a climate page.

Quest can match the substance. Quest has run this work since 1989 and holds eight written testimonials. What's missing is the page to put it on.

What Ventura has that Quest doesn't

Ventura's value is the shape, and it is the shape we should copy. Four service hubs — school, event, corporate, private tours — each with five to eight child pages underneath: camps, sports, swimming shuttles, AM/PM runs, conferences, Christmas parties, weddings, snow trips, social clubs. That is 45 pages doing 45 different jobs. It is also a business with 900 buses and 12 depots, so we are not out to out-scale them. We are out to out-specify them on the searches a Ferntree Gully operator can actually win.

Where the room is

Sporting clubs. Not one of the four has a page for club transport, despite every junior and senior club in Melbourne needing away-game buses through winter.

Wineries, from the venue's side. The wine-tour searches are fought over by tour operators selling tickets. Nobody is selling transport to the wineries and cellar doors that need it for their own guests. That is a business-to-business page with no competitor.

Area-level search. Every one of the four talks about “Melbourne” and stops. Quest's own lost keywords already prove demand in Pakenham, Shepparton, Traralgon and Echuca.

Vehicle-size search. Crown lists seat ranges inside a table. Nobody has a page built for “25 seater bus hire” the way people type it.

A note on ambition. Beating Ventura on “bus hire Melbourne” is a two-year job, not a six-month one. The sequence in §12 takes the winnable searches first — service, size and area terms where the competing pages are weak or absent — and uses the authority those build to go after the head terms later.

Why the hubs sit where they do

Three questions decide a charter booking: do you do my kind of trip, do you have a bus my size, and do you come to me. The three hubs answer one each, and every child page inherits authority from its hub while targeting its own search. A school sports coordinator and a winery events manager never have to read each other's page.

Deliberately not in the scope: a page per suburb. Fifty near-identical suburb pages is the oldest mistake in local search and Google treats it as what it is. Nine area pages go in, each with real detail — the schools, venues, clubs and freeway routes in that area, and where the depot sits relative to it. We add more only where Ahrefs and Search Console show demand, one at a time.

7 · Templates

Three templates. Thirty-six pages.

Building 36 pages one at a time is not affordable, and it is not necessary. Three templates are designed and built once, then each page is filled with its own research, copy, photos and schema. The structure repeats. The content never does.

Template A

Service page

23 pages · 900–1,400 words each. Targets one job-to-be-done, e.g. “school excursion bus hire Melbourne”.

  1. H1 naming the service and the city, plus a one-line promise
  2. Tappable call button and a three-field quote start, above the fold
  3. Who this is for, in that buyer's language
  4. What's included, and what it costs extra — answering the FAQ objections up front
  5. Which vehicle suits which group size, linking to the fleet pages
  6. The proof that matters to this buyer: accreditation for schools, invoicing for corporate, seatbelts for clubs
  7. Two or three named examples of real trips of this type
  8. Areas served, linking to the relevant area pages
  9. Four to six FAQs specific to this service, marked up as schema
  10. Review pulled from the matching testimonial, with review schema
  11. Closing quote block and phone number

Schema: Service + FAQPage + LocalBusiness + Breadcrumb.

Template B

Area page

9 pages · 700–900 words each. Targets “bus hire {area}” and the near-me searches from that area.

  1. H1 naming the area, with the depot distance stated plainly
  2. Tappable call button and quote start
  3. How Quest serves this area: pickup points, freeway routes, typical run times
  4. Suburbs and towns covered, written as a sentence not a keyword list
  5. Named local destinations Quest actually runs to from here
  6. The services most booked in this area, linking to Template A pages
  7. Which vehicles are based or most used on these runs
  8. One local job described in detail, with a review if we have one
  9. Three FAQs on travel time, minimum hire and out-of-area pricing
  10. Map and depot details

Each page needs 300+ words that could only be written about that area. If we can't write them, the page doesn't get built. Schema: LocalBusiness + areaServed + FAQPage.

Template C

Vehicle page

4 pages · 500–700 words each. Targets how people actually search: “25 seater bus hire”, “coaster bus hire”, “mini bus charter Melbourne”.

  1. H1 naming the seat count and the hire type
  2. Real photography of that vehicle, inside and out
  3. Spec table: seats, seatbelts, air conditioning, PA, luggage capacity, trailer option, accessibility
  4. What this vehicle is right for, and when to size up or down
  5. The trips it gets booked for most, linking to Template A pages
  6. Stated plainly: no onboard toilet, and what that means for long runs
  7. Quote start and phone number

The size-based searches Quest already ranked weakly for — 25-seater, 30-seater, coaster — land here. Schema: Product / Vehicle + Offer.

What makes this worth doing rather than spinning 36 near-identical pages

Google has spent three algorithm updates getting better at spotting templated pages with the nouns swapped. The template gives us the layout, the schema and the conversion furniture. The words, the photos, the named venues, the FAQs and the reviews are written per page from what Quest actually does. That is the difference between 36 assets and 36 liabilities, and it is the reason this is staged across twelve months rather than dumped live in one week.

10 · Trust content

Schools can't book what they can't verify.

This gets its own section because it is a revenue blocker, not an SEO nicety. Under Victorian Department of Education policy, a school chartering a bus has to satisfy itself that the operator is accredited with Safe Transport Victoria. Quest's website gives them nothing to check.

The about page says the business is “fully accredited”. There is no accreditation number, no mention of the Bus Safety Act, no child safety policy, no statement that drivers hold Working With Children checks, and no seatbelt information. A school business manager doing due diligence on three quotes will find all of that on Crown's site and none of it on Quest's.

Crown publishes a bus safety policy, a child safety and wellbeing policy, an environmental policy and a safety page, and names its accreditation number. Nuline, thin as it is, publishes a child safety policy. Quest publishes a 2020 COVID notice.

Three pages close this, and they are in phase 1 for that reason. They are also the pages that let the school, club and winery pages make claims that stand up.
