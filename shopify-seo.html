Shopify SEO

Your products don't rank. Your collections do.

The single biggest mistake in Shopify SEO is pouring effort into product pages. Nobody searches for your SKU — they search for the category it belongs to, and the page that wins that search is a collection page. Most stores ship with collections as bare product grids: no copy, no structure, no reason for Google to rank them. That's where the revenue is hiding, and it's where we start.

  • Collection architecture built from search demand. We map what Australians actually search in your category, then build the collection structure to match it — including the sub-collections most stores never create because Shopify doesn't prompt for them. One collection per commercial search intent, each with real copy that reads like a buying guide, not padding under a grid.
  • Shopify's duplicate-URL quirks, handled. Shopify publishes every product at two paths — its own URL and one nested under each collection — and canonicalises them itself, mostly correctly. Tag filters, vendor pages and variant parameters are where it goes wrong, quietly multiplying thin near-duplicate pages that dilute the pages you want ranking. We decide deliberately what gets indexed and what doesn't.
  • Product results that show price, stock and stars. Structured data and a clean Merchant Center feed so your listings carry price, availability and review ratings in Google — organic and Shopping surfaces both. On a results page where every competitor is plain blue text, that's the difference between being seen and being scrolled past.
  • Speed without gutting your theme. Most slow Shopify stores aren't slow because of Shopify — they're slow because of the apps. Every installed app injects its scripts on every page, including apps deactivated but never removed. We audit what's actually loading, cut what isn't earning its weight, and get Core Web Vitals to where rankings stop being penalised.
  • Content beyond the catalogue. Buying guides, comparisons and category explainers that catch shoppers a step before the purchase search — built on Shopify's blog, structured to feed authority to the collections they link into rather than sitting orphaned.
  • Reported in organic revenue. Rankings are the means. The report you get reads in sessions, conversion and dollars from organic search, against the same period last year. If organic revenue isn't growing, we don't hide behind position charts.

What that looks like when it works: $638K in organic revenue and first-page dominance in one of Australia's most competitive ecommerce categories, organic transactions up 309% for an ecommerce fencing retailer, and online sales revenue up 600% with top-3 rankings for a flower delivery brand.

The platform, honestly

Is Shopify actually good for SEO?

Good enough that the platform is never the excuse. Shopify handles the fundamentals better than most of what it replaced — clean canonical tags out of the box, automatic sitemaps, forced HTTPS, redirects managed for you when a URL changes. Stores that rank badly on Shopify would have ranked badly on anything, because the gap is almost never the platform. It's that nobody built the collection structure around search demand, wrote the copy, or watched what the theme and apps were doing to load times.

The honest limitations: you can't remove /products/ and /collections/ from your URLs, the blog is basic next to WordPress, and checkout is Shopify's territory, not yours. In fifteen years of doing this we're yet to see one of those cost a store a ranking it deserved. What does cost stores rankings is the long tail of tag-filter pages and near-identical variants Shopify generates silently — manageable, but only if someone is actually managing it.

A word on SEO apps, because every Shopify store owner has been pitched a dozen: an app can compress your images and tidy your meta tags, and the good ones are worth their monthly fee. What no app can do is decide which collections deserve to exist, write copy that ranks, or earn a link. If apps alone did the job, every store would rank — the app store is not a strategy.

Two situations where specialist help earns its keep fastest: migrations — moving to Shopify from WooCommerce, Magento or Neto without a full URL-by-URL redirect map is how stores lose years of accumulated equity in a weekend, and we've rebuilt enough post-migration wrecks to insist on doing it properly the first time. And out-of-stock and discontinued products — a store's default is to delete the page and lose everything it earned. The right answer depends on whether the product is coming back, has a successor, or is gone for good, and picking wrong at catalogue scale quietly bleeds authority.
